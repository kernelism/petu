# Golden lesson: won't the hotels just pass the tax on?

- **Document:** `openstax-principles-economics-3e.pdf` (OpenStax Principles of Economics 3e)
- **Page:** 139 ("Elasticity and Tax Incidence", Figure 5.10). Also uses p140 (wedge, tax revenue).
- **Question type:** misconception
- **Question (as the student types it):** "I don't get the hotel example. Won't hotels just add the tax onto the bill, so guests end up paying all of it?"

## The student's likely confusion

They picture price as something the seller simply chooses: add $50 to the bill and the guest pays it. That leaves out what happens to *quantity* at the new price. Guests who can go elsewhere stop booking, and rooms stand empty. They also tend to think that whoever legally hands the money to the government is who bears the tax. The page's rule ("the more inelastic side bears most") reads to them like a definition to memorise, not something that follows from what happens.

## Carrying example

A beach town's hotel rooms per night (the page's own beachfront-hotel case, given simple linear numbers so the split comes out round):

- Guests: Qd = 1000 − 4P (every $1 on the price loses 4 bookings: guests have other towns and other trips).
- Hotels: Qs = 200 + P (every $1 less removes only 1 room: the beach can't move).
- Before the tax: P = $160, Q = 360 rooms.
- Tax: $50 per room-night, collected from the hotels.
- After: guests pay Pc = $170, hotels keep Pp = $120, Q = 320 rooms. Guests carry $10 (20%), hotels $40 (80%). Revenue $16,000 per night.

The numbers are chosen so "pass it all on" can be tested directly ($210 leaves 200 empty rooms) and so the split is lopsided enough to see at a glance.

## The scene

**Things**
- A supply-and-demand plot, the largest object, left of centre: flat demand line D, steep supply line S, the equilibrium dot at ($160, 360).
- Two actor chips above the plot: **Guests** (top left) and **Hotels** (top right of the plot). Each has "escape route" arrows leaving it: Guests have a thick bundle labelled "4 fewer bookings per $1" (to "other towns, other trips"); Hotels a single thin arrow, "1 fewer room per $1" ("can't move the beach").
- A ghost dot at $210 (the "pass it all on" guess) with a gap bar showing 200 unsold rooms.
- The tax wedge: a vertical $50 bar fitted between D and S at Q = 320, top at $170 and bottom at $120.
- A split bar to the right of the plot: one $50 bar divided into $10 (guests) and $40 (hotels).
- Late in the lesson, a ghost demand line shifted down by $50 (the "charge guests instead" variant).

**Relations**
- Thick escape bundle → flat D line ("easy to walk away = flat"); thin escape arrow → steep S line.
- Price-axis gaps Pc − Pe and Pe − Pp → the two segments of the split bar (leader lines).
- Ghost demand line → the same wedge ("same split"), showing that who pays the government doesn't change the result.
- Escape-route thickness → split bar: the thin-escape side holds the bigger segment.

**Layout (in words)**
The plot dominates the left two-thirds and sits a little low, so the actor chips can sit above the curve they explain: Guests above the flat demand end, Hotels above the steep supply end. The escape arrows leave the chips *outward*, away from the plot, so they read as "ways out of this market". The split bar sits to the right at the height of the price axis gaps it explains, so its leader lines run horizontally from the price axis to the bar. The $210 ghost floats high above the equilibrium, deliberately outside the plot's comfortable range, so it looks wrong. Nothing is centred or mirrored: the plot is big, the bar is narrow and tall, and the chips are small.

## Beats

**1. Test the hunch on one town.**
- Say: "Your hunch is that hotels add $50 to the bill and guests pay it. Let's test it on one beach town. Before any tax, rooms go for $160 and 360 get booked a night."
- Board: the plot with D, S and the equilibrium dot at ($160, 360).
- Slides: p139 highlight "such as in the example of beachfront hotels", point at Figure 5.10 (a).
- Connections: Figure 5.10 (a) → board plot.

**2. Who can walk away?**
- Say: "Here's what matters. Raise the price a dollar and guests book four fewer rooms. They can go to another town. Cut what hotels get by a dollar and they offer only one fewer room. They can't move the beach."
- Board: Guests and Hotels chips with escape arrows (thick bundle, thin single). A leader line from the thick bundle to the flat demand line ("easy to walk away"), and from the thin arrow to the steep supply line.
- Slides: p139 highlight "sellers have no alternative than to accept lower prices".
- Connections: escape arrows → curves.

**3. Try passing it all on.**
- Say: "Suppose hotels really did pass it all on: guests pay $210 and hotels still keep $160. At $210, guests want only 160 rooms. At $160, hotels would happily fill 360. That's 200 empty rooms every night. That can't last."
- Board: ghost dot at $210 on the demand line; a horizontal gap bar from 160 to 360 rooms at the $160 supply level, labelled "200 empty rooms".
- Slides: none (stay on Figure 5.10).
- Connections: ghost dot → thick escape bundle ("this is where guests left").

**4. Where it settles.**
- Say: "Empty rooms push hotels to cut prices. Things settle where the $50 gap fits exactly between the two lines: guests pay $170, hotels keep $120, and 320 rooms get booked."
- Board: the ghost dot slides down the demand line to $170; the $50 wedge locks in between the curves at Q = 320, with Pc = $170 on top and Pp = $120 below. The $210 ghost fades but stays faintly.
- Slides: p140 highlight "The distance between Pc and Pp is the tax rate."
- Connections: p140 sentence → wedge.

**5. The split.**
- Say: "Compare with before. Guests pay $10 more than $160. Hotels keep $40 less. So hotels carry 80% of the tax, even though they were the ones who 'added it to the bill'."
- Board: the split bar grows to the right: $10 guests / $40 hotels, with leader lines from the Pc − Pe and Pe − Pp gaps on the price axis. A faint area on the plot: $50 × 320 = $16,000 a night to the town.
- Slides: p139 caption, highlight "Pc – Pe" and "Pe – Pp".
- Connections: caption terms → bar segments.

**6. It doesn't matter who hands over the money.**
- Say: "Now say the town charges guests the $50 directly instead. Every guest will now pay $50 less to the hotel, so demand drops by $50. Work it through and it lands in the same place: $120 to hotels, $170 all-in for guests."
- Board: ghost demand line shifted down $50 meets S at ($120, 320); the same wedge lights up; a note "same split" linking the ghost line to the split bar.
- Slides: none.
- Connections: ghost demand → split bar ("same split").

**7. The rule on the page.**
- Say: "That's the page's rule, and now you can see why: the side with fewer ways out pays more. Here it's the hotels. For cigarettes it flips, because smokers are the ones who can't walk away."
- Board: the thin escape arrow and the $40 segment pulse together; a connector from both to the p139 sentence.
- Slides: p139 highlight "sellers bear most of the tax burden", then point at "In the tobacco example".
- Connections: escape arrows + split bar → p139 rule.

## The aha

A tax lands on whoever can't walk away. Whether hotels or guests hand the money to the town doesn't change the split. Prices move until the $50 gap fits between the two curves, and the less flexible side absorbs most of that move.

## Check question

Cigarettes, per carton: smokers Qd = 1000 − P, sellers Qs = 200 + 4P, and a $50 tax collected from sellers. First guess who carries more, then find what smokers pay and what sellers keep.

**Answer:** Before the tax, P = $160 and Q = 840. After the tax, smokers pay $200 and sellers keep $150, with 800 cartons sold. Smokers carry $40 (80%) and sellers $10 (20%). It's the mirror image of the hotels: now demand has the fewer ways out.

## Why this is good

- Takes the student's wrong prediction seriously and *tests* it with the example ($210 leaves 200 empty rooms), rather than just asserting the right answer. The counterexample is aimed at this specific misconception.
- Elasticity is drawn as something you can see (escape routes of different thickness) and tied to the slopes of the curves, before the textbook word is used.
- One plot carries the whole lesson; later beats move dots and add a wedge and ghost curves on the same axes instead of opening new panels.
- Board-to-PDF connectors land on the page's exact symbols (Pc, Pe, Pp) and its rule, so the student can reread the page and recognise everything.
- The second counterfactual (charge guests instead) removes a second hidden misconception: that who legally pays decides who bears the tax.
- The check question flips which side is inflexible and asks for a prediction before the calculation.

## Appendix (hidden): arithmetic

- Equilibrium: 1000 − 4P = 200 + P → 800 = 5P → P = 160; Q = 1000 − 640 = 360 (supply: 200 + 160 = 360 ✓).
- Tax collected from hotels, t = 50: Pc − Pp = 50 and 1000 − 4Pc = 200 + Pp = 200 + (Pc − 50) = 150 + Pc → 850 = 5Pc → Pc = 170, Pp = 120. Q = 1000 − 680 = 320 (supply: 200 + 120 = 320 ✓).
- Burden: guests 170 − 160 = 10 (20%), hotels 160 − 120 = 40 (80%). Revenue 50 × 320 = 16,000.
- Formula check: consumer share = supply slope / (supply slope + |demand slope|) in dQ/dP terms = 1 / (1 + 4) = 0.2 ✓.
- Elasticities at the old equilibrium: demand 4 × 160/360 ≈ 1.78 (elastic); supply 1 × 160/360 ≈ 0.44 (inelastic) — consistent with Figure 5.10 (a).
- "Pass it all on" test: at Pc = 210, Qd = 1000 − 840 = 160; at Pp = 160, Qs = 360 → 200 unsold rooms.
- Tax on guests instead: guests' willingness falls by 50: 1000 − 4(Pp + 50) = 200 + Pp → 800 − 4Pp = 200 + Pp → 600 = 5Pp → Pp = 120, guests pay 170 all-in ✓.
- Check question: 1000 − P = 200 + 4P → P = 160, Q = 840. With tax: 1000 − Pc = 200 + 4(Pc − 50) = 4Pc → 1000 = 5Pc → Pc = 200, Pp = 150, Q = 800 (supply 200 + 600 = 800 ✓). Smokers 40, sellers 10.
- Simplification: the linear curves are invented for the page's qualitative hotel example. The narration presents them as "one beach town", not as the book's data.
